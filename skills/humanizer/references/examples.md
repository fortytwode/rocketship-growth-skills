# Editorial examples

All names, products and figures below are fictional. They illustrate editing decisions, not campaign recommendations or RocketShip client results. When editing real work, use its actual source context.

## Explain a technical finding without overstating it

Before: “Rank agreement is robust: Kendall tau is 0.82 and 0.88, and dropping any single creative moves Spearman by less than 0.03.”

Supplied context: two reports rank ads by purchase volume. Both use the same advertising platform's data. The author supplied these calculations; the editor has not independently verified them.

Report: “The two reports generally rank the ads similarly by purchase volume. The agreement changes little when we leave out any one ad, so it does not appear to depend on a single ad. Both reports use the same source data, so this does not independently confirm that the purchase counts are correct.”

Slide: “Both reports rank ads similarly by purchase volume.”

Supporting line when the slide is about reliability: “Both use the same source data; this does not validate purchase counts.”

Keep the supplied coefficients and method names in supporting methods material where needed. Do not turn purchase volume into profitability, or statistical agreement into verified attribution.

## Replace a slogan with the supplied meaning

Before: “One ritual. Endless possibility.”

Supplied context: the app lets a user choose a six-minute breathing exercise and set a reminder. No health or long-term outcome evidence is supplied.

After: “Choose a six-minute breathing exercise and set a reminder.”

Do not add “sleep better,” “reduce anxiety” or a promise of habit formation merely to make the sentence more persuasive.

## Answer the question the reader asked

Before: “There are several strategic considerations in balancing test breadth with the allocation of learning resources.”

Supplied context: the reader asked how many ads this specific test should use. The author's recommendation is four ads in each of two ad sets, at $80 a day per set. The author's reason is that the available budget would give additional ads too little spend to judge.

After: “For this test, we recommend four ads in each of two ad sets, at $80 a day per set. With this budget, adding more ads would give each one too little spend to judge.”

The numbers and rationale come from the supplied recommendation. They are not defaults for other accounts.

## Leave clear writing alone

Before: “The export covers April 1 to 14. It does not include April 15.”

After: unchanged.

## Flag a decision the editor cannot resolve

Before: “We have not confirmed which purchase count is correct. Switch all reporting to the new purchase event tomorrow.”

Response: “The draft says the purchase count is unconfirmed but also instructs readers to switch events. Should the switch wait for verification?”

The contradiction is visible in the draft. Do not choose an event or initiate an attribution audit as part of the edit.

## Keep missing evidence missing

Before: “The onboarding funnel exhibits substantial post-install attrition.”

Supplied context: many installers leave before finishing signup. No count, cause or remedy is supplied.

After: “Many people install the app but leave before finishing signup.”

Do not add a percentage, an explanation or a proposed fix.

## Use the length the material needs

Before: “Our creative testing strategy is entering an exciting new phase. We recommend testing two new creatives next week using the existing budget. This strategic approach will enable us to explore fresh possibilities while maintaining budget discipline.”

Slide: “Test two new creatives next week within the existing budget.”

Report: “We recommend testing two new creatives next week within the existing budget.”

The report stays short because the source supplies no further reasoning. Do not manufacture an explanation to make it feel more substantial.

## Explain the decision that accompanies a table

Supplied table, same reporting window and install definition:

| Creative | Spend | Installs | Cost per install |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Morning routine | $180 | 60 | $3 |
| Evening routine | $200 | 20 | $10 |

Supplied decision: hold both budgets steady until the trial data is available. No trial quality, profitability or causal explanation is established.

Before: “Morning routine delivered 60 installs on $180 of spend, with a cost per install of $3. Evening routine delivered 20 installs on $200 of spend, with a cost per install of $10. This is an exciting validation of our winning creative strategy.”

After: “Morning routine brought in installs at $3 each, compared with $10 for Evening routine. We will hold both budgets steady until the trial data is available.”

The comparison adds meaning; retyping every cell does not. The supplied next step stays intact. Cheaper installs do not establish a better business outcome.

## Keep a point edit narrow

Request: “Replace ‘unlock powerful synergies’ in this sentence. Keep the rest of the paragraph.”

Supplied sentence: “The shared calendar will unlock powerful synergies by showing both teams when a draft is ready for review.”

After: “The shared calendar will help both teams see when a draft is ready for review.”

Return the revised sentence. Do not rewrite the surrounding paragraph or start a new review process.
