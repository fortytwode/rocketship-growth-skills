# Humanizer

Make this sound like a capable colleague explaining their work to the intended reader. The central test is: **Could the author explain this, unprepared, in their own words?** Remove AI-sounding language in every format: inflated phrasing, empty slogans, forced contrasts, repeated three-part constructions and explanations that add nothing. Make the point and its basis easy to understand without saying more than the reader needs. This is an editing skill, not an analytical audit or a claim that the author's work has been verified.

## Work from the draft

Use the supplied text and relevant surrounding context. Infer audience and medium from the request and material; preserve the existing format when unspecified. Do not make choosing a medium an extra intake step. Do useful editing without an intake questionnaire. Ask a specific question only when missing meaning prevents a responsible rewrite, and continue with the rest.

Read [examples](references/examples.md) to calibrate substantial rewrites or technical explanations. Examples illustrate editorial choices, not instructions to reuse their facts or recommendations.

## Editorial priorities

- **Explain the meaning behind impressive language.** Turn a slogan into the actual action, finding or recommendation. “Three quests, one gate” becomes the requirement to link an account before using the quests, when the context establishes that meaning. Never invent what a metaphor was meant to conceal.
- **Make technical points explainable.** Say what was compared, what the result suggests and why the reader needs it. Keep a specialist term if this reader uses it or needs to learn it; explain it at first use when unfamiliar. If an acronym adds nothing for this reader, use its everyday meaning instead (for example, “verification code” rather than “OTP” in a client update). Method names and coefficients can live in a supporting note instead of leading a client update. Keep detail the reader needs to interpret the claim; don't turn a statistical result into a vague “the data looks good.”
- **Answer the question directly.** If the reader asked how many ads to run, lead with the supplied recommendation, then its reason. Don't replace an answer with a discussion of trade-offs. Don't manufacture an answer when none is supplied.
- **Use a natural professional voice.** Prefer concrete nouns and verbs, ordinary phrasing, and sentences someone could say aloud. Remove repeated dramatic fragments, forced three-part slogans, empty transitions and unnecessary recaps. Preserve personality and useful domain language. Don't add slang, filler, deliberate mistakes or generic friendliness to simulate a human.
- **Cut explanation that adds nothing.** Prefer the shortest natural version that carries the useful point. Remove restatements, scene-setting and conclusions that repeat the evidence. Expand only where the reader needs an explanation to understand the claim. Shortening a slogan into a punchier slogan has not solved the problem.
- **Write for a person, not a form.** A finding, answer or update written as labelled fields (Question: / Short answer: / Evidence: / Recommendation: / Confidence:) reads as machine output even when every field is true. Say it in three or four sentences the way the author would explain it across a table. The same goes for stance labels with a colon ("What is right and must not change:", "The nuance that keeps it honest:"), sentences that announce the order of what follows ("Three things, in this order, because each changes how you read the next"), candour openers ("I'll be straight") and verdict closers ("That is the standard."). Cut them; the content carries the weight.
- **Leave effective writing alone.** Don't rewrite merely to prove that editing happened. Never claim to detect AI authorship or promise to evade AI detectors.

## Fit the medium

The same plain, specific voice applies everywhere; the amount of detail changes.

- **Slides:** Lead with a concise takeaway. Use only the supporting text needed to read the slide; don't narrate a chart or repeat its title in a paragraph. Short phrases can be natural and useful. Keep essential qualifications visible; put optional method detail in a note when appropriate. Don't automatically add a paragraph or speaker notes to every slide.
- **Summary slides with several points:** Make each point a short labelled line, not a paragraph. The bold label should name what the line is (the problem, the plan, what works), one idea per line, about one line each. Each line should read on its own, without a "those" or "so" that depends on the line above. Before cutting a detail, check whether a later slide already carries it; keep what only this slide says, such as dates, the plan and the main findings.
- **Reports:** State the finding or recommendation first, then enough evidence and explanation to understand it. Reports permit depth, not repetition. Do not add speculative interpretation or a significance sentence to make a short finding feel like a report.
- **Updates sent with a table, chart or screenshot:** The table is the evidence; the text is the verdict. Don't read cells back. Lead with the answer in one sentence. Then one bullet per distinct finding, as many as there are findings and no more; each one compares or explains something the table can't say on its own. Give each bullet its context: what it is compared with (the target, last period or another segment) and, for any change we made, why we made it. Repeat a number only when you compare it with another. Lead with the metric the client pays for or the test was set up to move, not the easiest one to quote, such as CTR. Say when a result rests on small spend. End with a next step that names what happens, plus any open question; "continue monitoring" isn't a next step. If the table won't read on a phone, crop it to the rows that matter; don't retype it. In Slack and ClickUp, bold the headline's lead words and each bullet label. A label names the subject of its line (**Playable test**, **India:**, **Paused for performance:**, **Next:**), never a form field (Evidence:, Recommendation:).
- **Emails and replies to a prospect or client:** Answer what they asked, in the order they asked it, and keep their numbering if they used one. Each answer is a short paragraph or two or three bullets; the whole reply should read on a phone without the reader losing their place. Vary the shape between sections; six sections built the same way read as a template. If an answer needs a document (a sample page, a template, a data table), attach it, link it or offer to walk through it on the call. Do not paste it into the body. A thousand-word email that answers every question is still a wall of text; the reader asked for answers, not the report.
- **Slack updates:** Get to the change, decision or request. Include context the recipient needs to respond. A sentence may be enough; use bullets when several distinct items need attention. Skip formal introductions and closing recaps.

Use judgment, not fixed word or sentence quotas. A three-sentence source may become one sentence when the other two add no meaning. Retain useful specificity rather than replacing it with vague short copy.

Preserve the substance and certainty of what remains; keep retained numbers and named features accurate. Omit repetition and detail irrelevant to the requested medium without changing the claim. Do not add a separate logical-checking workflow. If an obvious inconsistency is visible in the supplied material, fix it when the correct meaning is unambiguous and briefly disclose a substantive correction. If resolving it requires evidence or a decision, flag that specific point instead of silently choosing an answer. Do not launch data pulls, fact-checking research or a full critique unless requested.

## Punctuation

Never use em dashes in rewritten output, including titles, headings, captions and examples. Use a comma, full stop or colon, or rewrite the sentence as appropriate; do not automatically substitute a hyphen.

## Return the useful result

Default: return one ready-to-use rewrite, without a scorecard or explanation of routine edits. Add a short note only for a material unresolved question or substantive correction. If the user asks for evaluation, examples or alternatives, show the relevant before/after passages and explain the editorial choice briefly.

Before returning, cut any phrase that merely restates the point or performs importance. Then read the result as natural language in its medium: can the intended reader follow the point, its reason and any essential uncertainty? Is it something a teammate could comfortably say? For slides and reports, also read it once as the client would: is it easy to scan, and could they say the main point back without rereading? Fix any label that doesn't match its line, any line that needs the one above it, and any list longer than the reader needs. Don't imply that rewriting certifies either the author's understanding or the truth of the analysis.

Return text unless the user asks to edit a file or document. The skill itself grants no permission to publish, send messages or change tasks.
